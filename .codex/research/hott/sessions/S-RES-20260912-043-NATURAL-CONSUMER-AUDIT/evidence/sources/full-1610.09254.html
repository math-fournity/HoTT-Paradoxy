Abstract

Capretta 2005’s delay monad can be used to model partial computations, but it has the “wrong” notion of built-in equality, strong bisimilarity. An alternative is to quotient the delay monad by the “right” notion of equality, weak bisimilarity. However, recent work by Chapman et al. 2015 suggests that it is impossible to define a monad structure on the resulting construction in common forms of type theory without assuming (instances of) the axiom of countable choice.

Using an idea from homotopy type theory—a higher inductive-inductive type—we construct a partiality monad without relying on countable choice. We prove that, in the presence of countable choice, our partiality monad is equivalent to the delay monad quotiented by weak bisimilarity. Furthermore we outline several applications.

1 Introduction

Computational effects can be modelled using monads, and in some functional programming languages (notably Haskell) they are commonly used as a program structuring device. In the presence of dependent types one can both write and reason about monadic programs. From a type theorist’s point of view, even a “pure” functional language like Haskell is not really pure as it has built-in effects, one of which is partiality: a function does not necessarily terminate. It is thus natural to look for a partiality monad which makes it possible to model partial computations and to reason about possibly non-terminating programs.

Capretta 2005 modeled partial computations using a coinductive construction that we call the delay monad [6]. We use the notation D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) for Capretta 2005’s type of delayed computations over a type AA. D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) is coinductively generated by 𝗇𝗈𝗐:A→D⁡(A)\mathsf{now}:A\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) and 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋:D⁡(A)→D⁡(A)\mathsf{later}:\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right). Examples of elements of D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) include 𝗇𝗈𝗐⁡(a)\mathsf{now}(a) and 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋⁡(𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋⁡(𝗇𝗈𝗐⁡(a)))\mathsf{later}(\mathsf{later}(\mathsf{now}(a))), as well as the infinitely delayed value ⊥\bot, defined by the guarded equation ⊥=𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋(⊥)\bot=\mathsf{later}(\bot). We can model recursive programs as Kleisli arrows A→D⁡(B)A\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(B\right), and we can construct fixpoints of (ω\omega-continuous) functions of type (A→D⁡(B))→(A→D⁡(B))(A\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(B\right))\to(A\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(B\right)), see Benton et al. 2009 [5].

Unfortunately, Capretta 2005’s delay monad is sometimes too intensional. It is often appropriate to treat two computations as equal if they terminate with the same value, but the delay monad allows us to count the number of “steps” (𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋\mathsf{later} constructors) used by a computation.

Capretta 2005 addressed this problem by defining a relation that we call weak bisimilarity, ∼D\sim_{\mathrm{D}}, and that relates expressions that only differ by a finite number of 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋\mathsf{later} constructors [6]. Capretta 2005 proved that the delay monad combined with weak bisimilarity is a monad in the category of setoids.

A setoid is a pair consisting of a type and an equivalence relation on that type. Setoids are sometimes used to approximate quotient types in type theories that lack support for quotients. However, a major difference between setoids and quotient types is that setoids do not provide a mechanism for information hiding. Using the setoid approach basically boils down to introducing a new relation together with the convention that all constructions have to respect this relation. A problem with this approach is that it can lead to something which has informally become known as setoid hell, in which one is forced to prove that a number of constructions—even some that do not depend on implementation details by, say, pattern matching on the 𝗇𝗈𝗐\mathsf{now} and 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋\mathsf{later} constructors—preserve setoid relations. This kind of problem does not afflict quotient types.

In a type theory with quotient types [14], one can consider using the quotient D(A)/∼D{\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)}/{\sim_{\mathrm{D}}} as the type of partial computations of type AA. This idea was discussed in a talk by Uustalu [7], reporting on joint work with Capretta 2005 and the first-named author of the current paper. However, the idea does not seem to work as intended. It is an open problem—and believed to be impossible—to show that this construction actually constitutes a monad (in “usual” forms of type theory).

With an additional assumption, Chapman et al. 2015 have managed to show that the partiality operator D(−)/∼D{\mathrm{D}\!\left(-\right)}/{\sim_{\mathrm{D}}} is a monad [8]. This additional assumption is known as countable choice. To express what this is, first note that the propositional truncation, written ‖−‖\left\lVert-\right\rVert and sometimes called “squashing”, is an operation that turns a type into a proposition (a type with at most one element). We can see ‖A‖\left\lVert A\right\rVert as the quotient of AA by the total relation. Countable choice says that Π\Pi and ‖−‖\left\lVert-\right\rVert commute if the domain is the natural numbers, in the sense that there is a function from Πn:ℕ‖P(n)‖\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\left\lVert P(n)\right\rVert to ‖Πn:ℕP(n)‖\left\lVert\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,P(n)\right\rVert. Even though this principle holds in some models, its status in type theory is unclear: the principle is believed to be independent of several variants of type theory. Recently Coquand et al. 2017 have shown that it cannot be derived in a theory with propositional truncation and a single univalent universe [10], speculating that the result might extend to a theory with a hierarchy of universes. Furthermore Richman 2001 argues that countable choice should be avoided in constructive reasoning [18]. The main purpose of the present paper is to define a partiality monad without making use of this principle.

The situation with the quotiented delay monad is similar to that of one variant of the real numbers in constructive mathematics. If the Cauchy reals are defined as a quotient, then it is impossible to prove a specific form of the statement that every Cauchy sequence of Cauchy reals has a limit using 𝖨𝖹𝖥Ref\mathsf{IZF}_{\mathrm{Ref}}, a constructive set theory without countable choice [17]. It is suspected that corresponding statements are also impossible to prove in several variants of type theory. An alternative solution was put forward in the context of homotopy type theory [20]. In that approach, the reals are constructed inductively simultaneously with a notion of closeness, and the quotienting is done directly in the definition using a higher inductive-inductive type (HIIT).

In 2015, Andrej Bauer and the first-named author of the current paper suggested to use a similar approach to define a partiality monad without using countable choice, an idea which was mentioned by Chapman et al. 2015 [8]. Here, we show that this is indeed possible.

We do not make use of the full power of HIITs, but restrict ourselves to set-truncated HIITs. We call such types quotient inductive-inductive types, QIITs, following Altenkirch and Kaposi 2016 [1]. Some of the theory of QIITs is developed in the forthcoming PhD thesis of Dijkstra 2017 [12], see also Altenkirch et al. 2016 [2]. Although type theory extended with QIITs is still experimental and currently lacks a solid foundation, QIITs are a significantly simpler concept than full-blown HIITs. It is conjectured that QIITs exist in some computational models of type theory.

The type theory that we work in can be described as a fragment of the theory considered in the standard textbook on homotopy type theory [20] (henceforth referred to as the HoTT book), and is quite close to the theory considered by Chapman et al. 2015 [8]. Details are given in Sect. 2. The construction of our partiality monad is given in Sect. 3, together with its elimination principle and some properties. Furthermore we show that it gives us free ω\omega-cpos in a sense that we will make precise. In Sect. 4 we show that, assuming countable choice, our partiality monad is equivalent to (in bijective correspondence to) the one of Chapman et al. 2015 [8]. We outline some applications of the partiality monad in Sect. 5, and conclude with a short discussion in Sect. 6.

Note that there are differences between the Agda code and the presentation in the text. In the formalisation the elimination principle is given as an induction principle, but we also prove that this principle is interderivable with the universal property. Finally there are a number of small differences between the formalisation and the text, and some results in the paper have not been formalised at all, most notably the results about the reals in Sect. 5.2.

2 Background: Type Theory with Quotient Inductive-Inductive Types

We work in intensional type theory of Martin-Löf style with all the usual components (e.g. Π\Pi, Σ\Sigma, inductive types), including the identity type (we use the notation x=yx=y). We assume that equality of functions is extensional, and that (strong) bisimilarity implies equality for coinductive types.

Chapman et al. 2015 [8] assume the axiom of uniqueness of identity proofs, UIP, for all small types (types in the lowest universe). UIP holds for a type AA if, for any elements xx, y:Ay:A, if we have equalities pp, q:x=yq:x=y, then we have p=qp=q. Instead of postulating an axiom, we prefer to work in a more general setting and restrict ourselves to types with the corresponding property. This approach is compatible with homotopy type theory. In the language of homotopy type theory, we work with sets or 00-truncated types; a type is a set if and only if it satisfies UIP. When we write A:𝖲𝖾𝗍A:\mathsf{Set}, we mean that AA is a type (in some universe) with the property of being a set; and when we write B:A→𝖲𝖾𝗍B:A\to\mathsf{Set}, we mean that BB is a family of types such that each B⁡(a)B(a) is a set.

Similarly to A:𝖲𝖾𝗍A:\mathsf{Set}, we write P:𝖯𝗋𝗈𝗉P:\mathsf{Prop} for a type PP with the property that it is a proposition, i.e. a (−1)(-1)-truncated type, i.e. a type with the property that any two of its elements are equal. A proposition is also a set. The type of all propositions in a certain universe is closed under all operations that are relevant to us, and the same applies to sets.

In addition to UIP, Chapman et al. 2015 [8] assume propositional extensionality—that logically equivalent propositions are equal—for all small propositions. This property is equivalent to the univalence axiom [20], restricted to (small) propositions. Just like Chapman et al. 2015, we only require propositional extensionality (not full univalence) for our development, with the exception that univalence is used to show that certain precategories (in the sense of the HoTT book [20]) are categories. For an example of how propositional extensionality is used, see Lemma 2.

Chapman et al. 2015 [8] also assume the existence of quotient types in the style of Hofmann 1995 [14]. Given a set AA and a propositional relation ∼\sim on it, the (set-) quotient A/∼{A}/{\sim} can in homotopy type theory be constructed as a higher inductive type with three constructors [20]:

[−]\displaystyle[-] :A→A/∼\displaystyle:A\to{A}/{\sim}
[−]=\displaystyle[-]^{=} :Πa,b:Aa∼b→[a]=[b]\displaystyle:\Pi_{a,b:A}\,a\sim b\to[a]=[b]
𝗂𝗋𝗋\displaystyle\mathsf{irr} :Πx,y:A/∼Πp,q:x=yp=q\displaystyle:\Pi_{x,y:{A}/{\sim}}\,\Pi_{p,q:x=y}\,p=q

The last constructor 𝗂𝗋𝗋\mathsf{irr} ensures that any two parallel equalities are equal, that is, that A/∼{A}/{\sim} is set-truncated. We call a higher inductive type with such a set-truncation constructor a quotient inductive type (QIT).

As noted above, Chapman et al. 2015 [8] use countable choice, which we want to avoid. Instead we make use of quotient inductive-inductive types (QIITs) [12; 2]. From the point of view of homotopy type theory, these are set-truncated higher inductive-inductive types (HIITs); some other examples of HIITs can be found in the HoTT book [20, Chap. 11]. While it seems plausible that QIITs exist in some computational models of type theory, this has yet to be determined.

3 The Partiality Monad

As indicated in the introduction we define the partiality monad (−)⊥(-)_{\bot} as a QIIT, defining the type A⊥A_{\bot} simultaneously with an ordering relation ⊑\sqsubseteq on A⊥A_{\bot}. We will first describe the constructors and the elimination principle of this definition, and then show that A⊥A_{\bot} is the underlying type of the free ω\omega-cpo (see Definition 2) on AA, thus proving that (−)⊥(-)_{\bot} is a monad. In the final part of this section we will give a characterisation of the ordering relation; this is perhaps not as trivial as one might expect, given the relation’s definition.

Note that our construction of A⊥A_{\bot} can be seen as a further example of a free algebraic structure defined in type theory. It was discussed in the HoTT book [20, Chap. 6.11] that free groups can be defined as (in our terminology) quotient inductive types, while it is well-known that even simpler examples can be defined as ordinary inductive types.

3.1 The Definition and Its Elimination Principles

Let AA be a set. We define the set A⊥A_{\bot} simultaneously with a binary propositional relation on A⊥A_{\bot}, written ⊑\sqsubseteq. The set A⊥A_{\bot} is generated by the following four constructors, plus a set-truncation constructor:

The constructor ⨆\bigsqcup is intended to form least upper bounds of increasing sequences, and α\alpha ensures that the ordering relation ⊑\sqsubseteq is antisymmetric.

The relation ⊑\sqsubseteq is a type family that is indexed twice by A⊥A_{\bot}. It is generated by six constructors. One of these constructors says that, for any x,y:A⊥x,y:A_{\bot}, the type x⊑yx\sqsubseteq y is a proposition (Πp,q:x⊑yp=q\Pi_{p,q:x\sqsubseteq y}\,p=q). The rules state that ⊑\sqsubseteq is reflexive and transitive, that ⊥\bot is at least as small as any other element of A⊥A_{\bot}, and that ⨆\bigsqcup constructs least upper bounds.

Now we will give the elimination principle of (−)⊥(-)_{\bot} and ⊑\sqsubseteq. This principle can be stated in different ways. One way would be to state it as an induction principle, along the following lines: Given a family P:A⊥→𝖲𝖾𝗍P:A_{\bot}\to\mathsf{Set} and […something for ⊑\sqsubseteq…], and given elements of P⁡(⊥)P(\bot), Πa:AP(η(a))\Pi_{a:A}\,P(\eta(a)), […and so on…], we can conclude that Πx:A⊥P(x)\Pi_{x:A_{\bot}}\,P(x) and […]. We take this approach in our formalisation; for another example, see the presentation of the Cauchy reals in the HoTT book [20, Chap. 11.3.2]. However, because the two types are defined simultaneously and involve constructors targeting the equality type, the induction principle may look somewhat involved and perhaps even ad-hoc, and it may not be obvious that it is the “correct” one.

Instead, we present a universal property. Dijkstra 2017 [12] and Altenkirch et al. 2016 [2] have worked out a general form and rules for a large class of quotient inductive-inductive types. In their setting, any specification of a QIIT gives rise to a category of algebras, following methods that have been used for W-types [4] and certain higher inductive types [19], and if this category has a (homotopy-) initial object, then this object is taken as the definition of the QIIT. We use the following algebras:

We can now make the elimination principle precise. Note that the tuple (A⊥,⊑,⊥,η,⨆)(A_{\bot},\sqsubseteq,\bot,\eta,\bigsqcup) is a partiality algebra. As the elimination principle of A⊥A_{\bot} and ⊑\sqsubseteq we take the statement that there is a unique (up to equality) morphism from this partiality algebra to any other partiality algebra over AA. In the terminology of the HoTT book [20], the statement that there is a morphism is basically the recursion principle of (−)⊥(-)_{\bot} and ⊑\sqsubseteq, while uniqueness gives us the power of the induction principle (with propositional computation rules). Note that allowing the type XX and the type family ⊑X\sqsubseteq_{X} to target arbitrary universes enables us to make use of large elimination.

We do not lose anything by using a universal property instead of an induction principle, at least for the induction principle referred to in the following theorem. The theorem is similar to results due to Dijkstra 2017 [12] and Altenkirch et al. 2016 [2]. It is stated without proof here, but a full proof of the fact can be found in our Agda development.

Theorem 3.1

The elimination principle of A⊥A_{\bot} and ⊑\sqsubseteq can be stated as an induction principle. This induction principle, which comes with propositional rather than definitional computation rules, is interderivable with the universal property given above.

Note that we could have defined A⊥A_{\bot} and ⊑\sqsubseteq differently. For instance, we could have omitted the set-truncation constructor from the definition of A⊥A_{\bot}, and then proved that the type is a set, following the approach taken for the Cauchy reals in the HoTT book [20]. However, if we had done this, then our definitions would have been less close to the general framework mentioned above [12; 2].

As a simple demonstration of the universal property we construct an induction principle for A⊥A_{\bot} that can be used when eliminating into a proposition. Following the terminology of the HoTT book [20, Chap. 11.3.2], we call it partiality induction:

Lemma 1

Let PP be a family of propositions on A⊥A_{\bot} such that both P⁡(⊥)P(\bot) and Πa:AP(η(a)){\Pi_{a:A}\,P(\eta(a))} hold. Assume further that, for any increasing sequence s:s: ℕ→A⊥\mathbb{N}\to A_{\bot} (with corresponding proof pp), Πn:ℕP(sn)\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,P(s_{n}) implies P⁡(⨆(s,p))P(\bigsqcup(s,p)). Then we can conclude Πx:A⊥P(x)\Pi_{x:A_{\bot}}\,P(x).

Proof

The proof uses a standard method. We define a partiality algebra where the set is Z:≡Σx:A⊥P(x)Z\vcentcolon\equiv\Sigma_{x:A_{\bot}}\,P(x); the binary relation is ⊑\sqsubseteq, ignoring the second projections of the values in ZZ; and the rest of the algebra is constructed using the assumptions. The universal property gives us a morphism mm from the initial partiality algebra to this one, and in particular a function of type A⊥→ZA_{\bot}\to Z. We are done if we can show that this function, composed with the first projection, is the identity on A⊥A_{\bot}. Note that the first projection can be turned into a partiality algebra morphism 𝖿𝗌𝗍\mathsf{fst}. Thus, by uniqueness, the composition of 𝖿𝗌𝗍\mathsf{fst} and mm has to be the unique morphism from the initial partiality algebra to itself, and the function component of this morphism is the identity. ∎

3.2 ω\omega-Complete Partial Orders

Another way of characterising our quotient inductive-inductive partiality monad is to say that A⊥A_{\bot} is the free (pointed) ω\omega-cpo over AA:

Definition 2

Let us denote the category 𝖯𝖺𝗋𝗍𝟎\mathsf{Part}_{\mathbf{0}}, where 𝟎\mathbf{0} is the empty type, by ω​-​𝖢𝖯𝖮\mathsf{\omega\textsf{-}CPO}. An ω\omega-cpo is an object of this category.

Let us quickly check that this definition makes sense. A partiality algebra on 𝟎\mathbf{0} is a set XX with a binary propositional relation ⊑X\sqsubseteq_{X} that is a partial order. After proving some equalities we end up with the following result, where the definition of “adjoint” is taken from the HoTT book [20, Definition 9.3.1]:

Theorem 3.2

For a given set AA, the functor 𝖥\mathsf{F} is a left adjoint to the forgetful functor 𝖴\mathsf{U}. This means that 𝖥⁡(A)\mathsf{F}(A) can be seen as the free ω\omega-cpo over AA. ∎

Thus we get a justification for calling the concept that we are discussing the partiality monad:

Corollary 1

The composition 𝖴∘𝖥:𝖲𝖤𝖳→𝖲𝖤𝖳\mathsf{U}\circ\mathsf{F}:\mathsf{SET}\to\mathsf{SET}, which maps objects AA to A⊥A_{\bot}, is a monad. ∎

Note that one can also construct a monad structure on (−)⊥(-)_{\bot} directly. Let us fix the set AA. The unit is given by η\eta. For the multiplication μ:(A⊥)⊥→A⊥\mu:(A_{\bot})_{\bot}\to A_{\bot}, note that A⊥A_{\bot} can be given the structure of a partiality algebra over A⊥A_{\bot} in a trivial way: the underlying set is A⊥A_{\bot}, the function ηA⊥:A⊥→A⊥\eta_{A_{\bot}}:A_{\bot}\to A_{\bot} is the identity, ⊑A⊥\sqsubseteq_{A_{\bot}} is ⊑\sqsubseteq, and so on. This gives us the function μ\mu as the unique morphism from the initial partiality algebra to this one. Proving the monad laws is straightforward.

3.3 A Characterisation of the Relation ⊑\sqsubseteq

To further analyse the QIIT construction, we show how the relation ⊑\sqsubseteq on the set A⊥A_{\bot} behaves.11 1 The work presented in Sect. 3.3 was done in collaboration with Paolo Capriotti. These results are useful when working with the partiality monad, and will play an important role in the next section of the paper. The arguments are only sketched here, details are given in the formalisation. We use the propositional truncation ‖−‖\left\lVert-\right\rVert (also known as “squashing”), which turns a type into a proposition. It can be implemented by quotienting with the trivial relation.

We know that ⊥⊑y\bot\sqsubseteq y is (by definition) satisfied for any y:A⊥y:A_{\bot}, and for the least upper bound we have that ⨆(s,q)⊑y\bigsqcup(s,q)\sqsubseteq y is equivalent to Πn:ℕsn⊑y\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,s_{n}\sqsubseteq y. The following lemma provides a characterisation of η⁡(a)⊑y\eta(a)\sqsubseteq y, for any a:Aa:A:

Lemma 2

The binary relation ⊑\sqsubseteq on A⊥A_{\bot} has the following properties:

η⁡(a)\displaystyle\eta(a) ⊑⁣⊥\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\bot ↔\displaystyle\leftrightarrow 𝟢\displaystyle\mathsf{0}
η⁡(a)\displaystyle\eta(a) ⊑η⁡(b)\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\eta(b) ↔\displaystyle\leftrightarrow a=b\displaystyle a=b
η⁡(a)\displaystyle\eta(a) ⊑⨆(s,q)\displaystyle\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup(s,q) ↔\displaystyle\leftrightarrow ‖Σn:ℕη(a)⊑sn‖\displaystyle\left\lVert\Sigma_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\eta(a)\sqsubseteq s_{n}\right\rVert

We will give the proof of this lemma later and make a remark first. Constructors in “HIT-like” definitions, e.g. QIITs, may in general be neither injective nor disjoint. For instance, ⨆(λn.⊥,q)=⊥\bigsqcup(\lambda n.\bot,q)=\bot. However, we have the following lemma:

Corollary 2

For any a:Aa:A and y:A⊥y:A_{\bot}, we have that η⁡(a)⊑y\eta(a)\sqsubseteq y implies that η⁡(a)=y\eta(a)=y. Furthermore η\eta is injective: if η⁡(a)=η⁡(b)\eta(a)=\eta(b), then a=ba=b. Moreover we have η(a)≠⊥\eta(a)\not=\bot.

Proof (of Corollary 2)

The last two claims are simple consequences of the lemma and reflexivity. For the first claim, let us fix a:Aa:A and apply Lemma 1 with P⁡(y):≡η⁡(a)⊑y→η⁡(a)=yP(y)\vcentcolon\equiv\eta(a)\sqsubseteq y\to\eta(a)=y. The only non-immediate step is the case for ⨆(s,q)\bigsqcup(s,q), where we can assume Πn:ℕP(sn)\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,P(s_{n}). From η⁡(a)⊑⨆(s,q)\eta(a)\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup(s,q) and Lemma 2 we get ‖Σn:ℕη(a)⊑sn‖\left\lVert\Sigma_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\eta(a)\sqsubseteq s_{n}\right\rVert. We are proving a proposition, so we can assume that we have n:ℕn:\mathbb{N} such that η⁡(a)⊑sn\eta(a)\sqsubseteq s_{n}. This implies that, for all m≥nm\geq n, η⁡(a)⊑sm\eta(a)\sqsubseteq s_{m} and hence, by the “inductive hypothesis”, η⁡(a)=sm\eta(a)=s_{m}. Thus η⁡(a)\eta(a) is an upper bound of ss, so we get ⨆(s,q)⊑η⁡(a)\bigsqcup(s,q)\sqsubseteq\eta(a), which by antisymmetry implies ⨆(s,q)=η⁡(a)\bigsqcup(s,q)=\eta(a). ∎

The proof of the lemma is more technical. The approach is similar to that used to prove some results about the real numbers defined as a HIIT in the HoTT book [20, Theorems 11.3.16 and 11.3.32]. We only give a sketch here, the complete proof can be found in our Agda formalisation.

Proof (of Lemma 2)

For every a:Aa:A we construct a relation in A⊥→𝖯𝗋𝗈𝗉A_{\bot}\to\mathsf{Prop} by applying the elimination principle of A⊥A_{\bot} and ⊑\sqsubseteq, treating 𝖯𝗋𝗈𝗉\mathsf{Prop} as a partiality algebra over AA in the following way:

Propositional extensionality is used to prove that 𝖯𝗋𝗈𝗉\mathsf{Prop} is a set (this is a variant of an instance of Theorem 7.1.11 in the HoTT book [20]), and to prove the antisymmetry law.

Using Lemma 1 one can then show that the defined relation is pointwise equal to η(a)⊑−\eta(a)\sqsubseteq-, and it is easy to see that the relation has the properties claimed in the statement of Lemma 2. ∎

4 Relation to the Coinductive Construction

In this section we compare our QIIT to Capretta 2005’s coinductive delay monad [6], quotiented by weak bisimilarity [8]. Let us start by giving Capretta 2005’s construction, as already outlined in the introduction. 𝒰\mathcal{U} stands for a universe of types.

Definition 3 (delay monad and weak bisimilarity)

For a set AA the delay monad D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) is the coinductive type generated by 𝗇𝗈𝗐:A→D⁡(A)\mathsf{now}:A\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) and 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋:D⁡(A)→D⁡(A)\mathsf{later}:\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right). The “terminates with” relation ↓D:D(A)→A→𝒰\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}\ :\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)\to A\to\mathcal{U} is the indexed inductive type generated by two constructors of type η(a)↓Da\eta(a)\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}a and p↓Da→𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋(p)↓Dap\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}a\to\mathsf{later}(p)\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}a. Furthermore, xx and y:D⁡(A)y:\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) are said to be weakly bisimilar, written x∼Dyx\sim_{\mathrm{D}}y, if Πa:Ax↓Da↔y↓Da\Pi_{a:A}\,x\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}a\leftrightarrow y\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}a holds.

It is easy to give D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) the structure of a monad. Note that x↓Dax\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}a can alternatively be defined to be Σn:ℕx=𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋n(𝗇𝗈𝗐(a))\Sigma_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,x=\mathsf{later}^{n}(\mathsf{now}(a)). The types x↓Dax\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}}a and x∼Dyx\sim_{\mathrm{D}}y are propositional, and ∼D\sim_{\mathrm{D}} is an equivalence relation on D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right).

The goal of this section is to show that, in the presence of countable choice, the partiality monad A⊥A_{\bot} is equivalent to D(A)/∼D{\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)}/{\sim_{\mathrm{D}}}. (We use the notion of equivalence from the HoTT book [20], which for sets is equivalent to bijective correspondence.) To understand the structure of the proof, let us observe that D(A)/∼D{\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)}/{\sim_{\mathrm{D}}} is constructed as a “coinductive type that is quotiented afterwards”, while A⊥A_{\bot} is an “inductive type that is quotiented at the time of construction”. To build a connection between these, it seems rather intuitive to consider an intermediate construction, either a “coinductive type that is quotiented at the time of construction” or an “inductive type that is quotiented afterwards”. The theory of “higher coinductive types” has, as far as we know, not been explored much yet, so we go with the second option. We do not even need an inductive construction: it is well-known that coinductive structures can be represented using finite approximations, and here, it is enough to consider monotone functions. Thus, first we will show that D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) is equivalent to a type of monotone sequences, carefully formulated, and that the equivalence lifts to the quotients. Then we will prove that, assuming countable choice, the quotiented monotone sequences are equivalent to A⊥A_{\bot}.

4.1 The Delay Monad and Monotone Sequences

For a set AA we say that a function g:ℕ→A+𝟏g:\mathbb{N}\to A+\mathbf{1} is a monotone sequence if it satisfies the propositional property

𝗂𝗌𝗆𝗈𝗇(g):≡Πn:ℕ(gn=gn+1)+((gn=𝗂𝗇𝗋(⋆))×(gn+1≠𝗂𝗇𝗋(⋆))).\mathsf{ismon}(g)\;\vcentcolon\equiv\;\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\left(g_{n}=g_{n+1}\right)+\left((g_{n}=\mathsf{inr}(\star))\times(g_{n+1}\not=\mathsf{inr}(\star))\right).

The set of monotone sequences, Σg:ℕ→A+𝟏𝗂𝗌𝗆𝗈𝗇(g)\Sigma_{g:\mathbb{N}\to A+\mathbf{1}}\,\mathsf{ismon}(g), is denoted by 𝖲𝖾𝗊A\mathsf{Seq}_{A}. Below the notation −n-_{n} will be used not only for functions, but also for monotone sequences; (g,p)n(g,p)_{n} means gng_{n}.

As Chapman et al. 2015 [8] observe, one can construct a sequence of type ℕ→A+𝟏\mathbb{N}\to A+\mathbf{1} from an element of D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right). If their construction is tweaked a little, then the resulting sequences are monotone, and the map is an equivalence:

Lemma 3

The types 𝖲𝖾𝗊A\mathsf{Seq}_{A} and D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) are equivalent.

Proof

We can simply give functions back and forth. Note that endofunctions on D⁡(A)\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) that correspond to 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋\mathsf{later} and “remove 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋\mathsf{later}, if there is one” can be mimicked for 𝖲𝖾𝗊A\mathsf{Seq}_{A}: let us use the names 𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑓𝑡\mathit{shift} and 𝑢𝑛𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑓𝑡:𝖲𝖾𝗊A→𝖲𝖾𝗊A\mathit{unshift}:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}\to\mathsf{Seq}_{A} for the functions that are determined by 𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑓𝑡​(g)0:≡𝗂𝗇𝗋⁡(⋆)\mathit{shift}(g)_{0}\vcentcolon\equiv\mathsf{inr}(\star), 𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑓𝑡​(g)n+1:≡gn\mathit{shift}(g)_{n+1}\vcentcolon\equiv g_{n}, and 𝑢𝑛𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑓𝑡​(g)n:≡gn+1\mathit{unshift}(g)_{n}\vcentcolon\equiv g_{n+1}.

Define j:D⁡(A)→𝖲𝖾𝗊Aj:\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)\to\mathsf{Seq}_{A} such that j⁡(𝗇𝗈𝗐⁡(a))j(\mathsf{now}(a)) equals λ​n.𝗂𝗇𝗅⁡(a)\lambda n.\mathsf{inl}(a), and j⁡(𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋⁡(x))j(\mathsf{later}(x)) equals 𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑓𝑡⁡(j⁡(x))\mathit{shift}(j(x)). One way to do this is to define j​(z)nj(z)_{n} by recursion on nn, followed by case distinction on zz. Furthermore, define h:𝖲𝖾𝗊A→D⁡(A)h:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}\to\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right) in the following way: Given s:𝖲𝖾𝗊As:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}, do case distinction on s0s_{0}. If s0s_{0} is 𝗂𝗇𝗅⁡(a)\mathsf{inl}(a), return 𝗇𝗈𝗐⁡(a)\mathsf{now}(a). Otherwise, return 𝗅𝖺𝗍𝖾𝗋⁡(h⁡(𝑢𝑛𝑠ℎ𝑖𝑓𝑡⁡(s)))\mathsf{later}(h(\mathit{unshift}(s))). It is straightforward to show that jj and hh are inverses of each other. ∎

As an aside, our formalisation shows that Lemma 3 holds even if AA is not a set.

Next, we mimic the relation ↓D\downarrow_{\mathrm{D}} by setting

↓𝖲𝖾𝗊:𝖲𝖾𝗊A→A→𝒰\displaystyle{\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}}:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}\to A\to\mathcal{U}
s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a:≡Σn:ℕsn=𝗂𝗇𝗅(a).\displaystyle s\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\;\vcentcolon\equiv\;\Sigma_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,s_{n}=\mathsf{inl}(a).

The relation ↓𝖲𝖾𝗊\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}} is not in general propositional. To remedy this, we can truncate and consider ‖s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a‖\left\lVert s\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\right\rVert. Using strategies explained by Kraus et al. 2013 [15], we have ‖s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a‖→s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a\left\lVert s\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\right\rVert\to s\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a, so we can always extract a concrete value of nn: a variant of the definition above in which the number nn is required to be minimal is propositional, and this definition can be shown to be logically equivalent to both ‖s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a‖\left\lVert s\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\right\rVert and s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊as\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a. See the formalisation for details.

We define the propositional relations ⊑𝖲𝖾𝗊\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{Seq}{}} and ∼𝖲𝖾𝗊\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}} by

By checking that the equivalence from Lemma 3 maps ∼𝖲𝖾𝗊\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}-related elements to ∼D\sim_{\mathrm{D}}-related elements, we get:

Lemma 4

The sets 𝖲𝖾𝗊A/∼𝖲𝖾𝗊{\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}/{\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}} and D(A)/∼D{\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)}/{\sim_{\mathrm{D}}} are equivalent. ∎

4.2 Monotone Sequences and the QIIT Construction

As the final step of showing that D(A)/∼D{\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)}/{\sim_{\mathrm{D}}} and A⊥A_{\bot} are equivalent, we show that 𝖲𝖾𝗊A/∼𝖲𝖾𝗊{\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}/{\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}} and A⊥A_{\bot} are. The plan is as follows: There is a canonical function w:𝖲𝖾𝗊A→A⊥w:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}\to A_{\bot} which can be extended to a function w~:𝖲𝖾𝗊A/∼𝖲𝖾𝗊→A⊥\tilde{w}:{\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}/{\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}}\to A_{\bot}. The function w~\tilde{w} is injective. Furthermore, if we assume countable choice, then the function ww, and thus also w~\tilde{w}, are surjective. Thus w~\tilde{w} is an equivalence.

Let us start by constructing ww and w~\tilde{w}. We use a copairing function [η∣⊥]:A+𝟏→A⊥\left[\eta\mid\bot\right]:A+\mathbf{1}\to A_{\bot} defined by [η∣⊥](𝗂𝗇𝗅(a)):≡η(a)\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](\mathsf{inl}(a))\vcentcolon\equiv\eta(a) and [η∣⊥](𝗂𝗇𝗋(⋆)):≡⊥\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](\mathsf{inr}(\star))\vcentcolon\equiv\bot, and define w:𝖲𝖾𝗊A→A⊥w:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}\to A_{\bot} by w(s,q):≡⨆([η∣⊥]∘s,…)w(s,q)\vcentcolon\equiv\bigsqcup(\left[\eta\mid\bot\right]\circ s,\ldots), with a canonical proof of monotonicity.

Lemma 5

The function ww is monotone: Πs,t:𝖲𝖾𝗊As⊑𝖲𝖾𝗊t→w(s)⊑w(t)\Pi_{s,t:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}\,s\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}t\to w(s)\sqsubseteq w(t). Thus ww extends to a map w~:𝖲𝖾𝗊A/∼𝖲𝖾𝗊→A⊥\tilde{w}:{\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}/{\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}}\to A_{\bot}.

Proof

For the second claim we show that ww maps elements related by ∼𝖲𝖾𝗊\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}} to equal elements. This follows from the first claim by antisymmetry. For the first claim it suffices to find a function k:ℕ→ℕk:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N} such that, for all nn, we have [η∣⊥](sn)⊑[η∣⊥](tk⁡(n))\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](s_{n})\sqsubseteq\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](t_{k(n)}). Fix nn. If sns_{n} is 𝗂𝗇𝗋⁡(⋆)\mathsf{inr}(\star), then [η∣⊥](sn)\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](s_{n}) is ⊥\bot, and k⁡(n)k(n) can thus be chosen arbitrarily. If sns_{n} is 𝗂𝗇𝗅⁡(a)\mathsf{inl}(a), then we have s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊as\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a and therefore t↓𝖲𝖾𝗊at\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a, which gives us a number k⁡(n)k(n) such that tk⁡(n)=𝗂𝗇𝗅⁡(a)t_{k(n)}=\mathsf{inl}(a) and [η∣⊥](sn)=η(a)=[η∣⊥](tk⁡(n))\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](s_{n})=\eta(a)=\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](t_{k(n)}). ∎

Lemma 6

The function w~\tilde{w} is injective: Πs,t:𝖲𝖾𝗊A/∼𝖲𝖾𝗊w~(s)=w~(t)→s=t\Pi_{s,t:{\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}/{\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}}}\,\tilde{w}(s)=\tilde{w}(t)\to s=t.

Proof

It suffices to show that, for s,t:𝖲𝖾𝗊As,t:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}, w⁡(s)=w⁡(t)w(s)=w(t) implies s∼𝖲𝖾𝗊ts\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}t. By symmetry, it is enough to fix a:Aa:A and show ‖s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a‖→‖t↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a‖\left\lVert s\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\right\rVert\to\left\lVert t\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\right\rVert, which follows from s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a→‖t↓𝖲𝖾𝗊a‖s\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\to\left\lVert t\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a\right\rVert. If s↓𝖲𝖾𝗊as\downarrow_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}a then w⁡(s)=η⁡(a)w(s)=\eta(a), and thus also w⁡(t)=η⁡(a)w(t)=\eta(a). Using Lemma 2 and Corollary 2 we then get ‖Σn:ℕη(a)=[η∣⊥](tn)‖\left\lVert\Sigma_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\eta(a)=\left[\eta\mid\bot\right](t_{n})\right\rVert, which implies ‖Σn:ℕtn=𝗂𝗇𝗅(a)‖\left\lVert\Sigma_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,t_{n}=\mathsf{inl}(a)\right\rVert. ∎

Lemma 7

Under countable choice, ww is surjective: Πx:A⊥‖Σs:𝖲𝖾𝗊Aw(s)=x‖\Pi_{x:A_{\bot}}\,\left\lVert\Sigma_{s:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}\,w(s)=x\right\rVert.

Proof

We apply the simplified induction principle presented in Lemma 1. The propositional predicate is P(x):≡‖Σs:𝖲𝖾𝗊Aw(s)=x‖P(x)\vcentcolon\equiv\left\lVert\Sigma_{s:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}\,w(s)=x\right\rVert. Both P⁡(⊥)P(\bot) and P⁡(η⁡(a))P(\eta(a)) are trivial: in the first case we use the sequence that is constantly 𝗂𝗇𝗋⁡(⋆)\mathsf{inr}(\star), while in the second case we take the one that is constantly 𝗂𝗇𝗅⁡(a)\mathsf{inl}(a).

The interesting part is to show P⁡(⨆(f,p))P\left(\bigsqcup(f,p)\right) for a given f:ℕ→A⊥f:\mathbb{N}\to A_{\bot} and p:Πn:ℕfn⊑fn+1p:\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,f_{n}\sqsubseteq f_{n+1}. By the mentioned induction principle, we can assume Πn:ℕP(fn)\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,P(f_{n}), which unfolds to Πn:ℕ‖Σt:𝖲𝖾𝗊Aw(t)=fn‖\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\left\lVert\Sigma_{t:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}\,w(t)=f_{n}\right\rVert. By countable choice, we can swap Πn:ℕ\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}} and ‖−‖\left\lVert-\right\rVert, which allows us to remove the truncation completely, because the goal is propositional. Hence we can assume Πn:ℕΣt:𝖲𝖾𝗊Aw(t)=fn\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,\Sigma_{t:\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}\,w(t)=f_{n}.

Using the usual distributivity law for Π\Pi\, and Σ\Sigma\, (sometimes called the “type-theoretic axiom of choice”), we can assume that we are given g:ℕ→𝖲𝖾𝗊Ag:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Seq}_{A} and a proof γ:Πn:ℕw(gn)=fn\gamma:\Pi_{n:\mathbb{N}}\,w(g_{n})=f_{n}. This immediately shows that w~\tilde{w} is surjective as well. Putting the pieces together, we get the main result of this section:

Theorem 4.1

In the presence of countable choice the map w~\tilde{w} is an equivalence. Hence the three sets D(A)/∼D{\mathrm{D}\!\left(A\right)}/{\sim_{\mathrm{D}}}, 𝖲𝖾𝗊A/∼𝖲𝖾𝗊{\mathsf{Seq}_{A}}/{\sim_{\mathsf{Seq}{}}} and A⊥A_{\bot} are equivalent.

Proof

A function between sets is an equivalence exactly if it is surjective and injective. This is a special case of Theorem 4.6.3 in the HoTT book [20], which states that a function between arbitrary types is an equivalence if and only if it is surjective and an embedding, which for sets is equivalent to being injective. ∎

5 Applications

The following examples show that our construction can be used in formalisations.

5.1 Nonterminating Functions as Fixed Points

Partiality algebras can be used to implement not necessarily terminating functions. Let (Y,⊑Y,⊥Y,ηY,⨆Y)(Y,\sqsubseteq_{Y},\bot_{Y},\eta_{Y},\bigsqcup_{Y}) be a partiality algebra, and let φ:Y→Y\varphi:Y\to Y be a monotone and ω\omega-continuous function. We can write down the least fixed point of φ\varphi directly as ⨆Y(λn.φn(⊥Y),p)\bigsqcup_{Y}(\lambda n.\varphi^{n}(\bot_{Y}),p), where pp is constructed from the fact that ⊥Y⊑Yφ(⊥Y)\bot_{Y}\sqsubseteq_{Y}\varphi(\bot_{Y}) and from the monotonicity proof of φ\varphi. One does not need ω\omega-continuity to write down this expression, but we use it to prove that the expression is a fixed point of φ\varphi.

If (Y,⊑Y,⊥Y,ηY,⨆Y)(Y,\sqsubseteq_{Y},\bot_{Y},\eta_{Y},\bigsqcup_{Y}) is a partiality algebra and XX is any type, then the function space X→YX\to Y can be given the structure of a partiality algebra in a canonical way (this is done for dependent types Πx:XY(x)\Pi_{x:X}\,Y(x) in the formalisation). As an example of how this kind of partiality algebra can be used we will construct a function 𝑠𝑒𝑎𝑟𝑐ℎq:Aω→A⊥\mathit{search}_{q}:A^{\omega}\to A_{\bot} that takes an element of the coinductive set of streams AωA^{\omega} and searches for an element of the set AA satisfying the decidable predicate q:A→𝟐q:A\to\mathbf{2}. The function is constructed as the least fixed point of the following endofunction on Aω→A⊥A^{\omega}\to A_{\bot}:

Φ⁡(f)​(a::𝑎𝑠):≡𝗂𝖿​q​(a)​𝗍𝗁𝖾𝗇​η​(a)​𝖾𝗅𝗌𝖾​f​(𝑎𝑠)\displaystyle\Phi(f)(a\dblcolon\mathit{as})\;\vcentcolon\equiv\;\mathsf{if}\ q(a)\ \mathsf{then}\ \eta(a)\ \mathsf{else}\ f(\mathit{as})

It is straightforward to check that f⊑gf\sqsubseteq g implies Φ⁡(f)⊑Φ⁡(g)\Phi(f)\sqsubseteq\Phi(g) by applying Φ⁡(f)\Phi(f) and Φ⁡(g)\Phi(g) to a point a::𝑎𝑠a\dblcolon\mathit{as} and doing case analysis on q⁡(a)q(a). Thus Φ\Phi is monotone. In a similar way one can verify that Φ\Phi is ω\omega-continuous.

5.2 Functions from the Reals

Let us consider the Cauchy reals, defined as a quotient. We say that f:ℕ→ℚf:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q} is a Cauchy sequence if, for all mm, n:ℕn:\mathbb{N} with m<nm<n, we have −1<m⋅(fm−fn)-1<m\cdot(f_{m}-f_{n}) <1<1. Furthermore, ff and gg are equivalent (written f∼gf\sim g) if, for all n:ℕn:\mathbb{N}, we have −2≤-2\leq n⋅(fn−gn)≤2n\cdot(f_{n}-g_{n})\leq 2. We use the notation ℝq\mathbb{R}^{q} for the quotient of Cauchy sequences by ∼\sim.

A meta-theoretic result is that, without further assumptions, any definable function (i.e. any closed term) of type ℝq→𝟐\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbf{2} is constant for reasons of continuity [16]. In particular, we cannot define a function 𝑖𝑠𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑒\mathit{isPositive} which checks whether a real number is positive. However, we can define a function 𝑖𝑠𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑒:ℝq→𝟐⊥\mathit{isPositive}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbf{2}_{\bot} such that 𝑖𝑠𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑒⁡(r)\mathit{isPositive}(r) is equal—but not judgmentally/definitionally equal—to η⁡(1𝟐)\eta(1_{\mathbf{2}}) if rr is positive, η⁡(0𝟐)\eta(0_{\mathbf{2}}) if rr is negative, and ⊥\bot if rr is zero.

We define this function as follows: Given a Cauchy sequence f:ℕ→ℚf:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Q}, we construct a new sequence f¯:ℕ→{−,?,+}\overline{f}:\mathbb{N}\to\{-,?,+\}. The idea is that f¯n\overline{f}_{n} is an approximation which only takes fif_{i} with i≤ni\leq n into account. We start with f¯0:≡?\overline{f}_{0}\vcentcolon\equiv\;?. If we have chosen f¯n−1\overline{f}_{n-1} to be −-, then we choose f¯n\overline{f}_{n} to be −- as well, and analogously for ++. If we have chosen f¯n−1\overline{f}_{n-1} to be ??, we check whether fn⋅n<−2f_{n}\cdot n<-2, in which case we choose f¯n\overline{f}_{n} to be −-; if fn⋅n>2f_{n}\cdot n>2, we choose f¯n\overline{f}_{n} to be ++; otherwise, we choose f¯n\overline{f}_{n} to be ??. We can compose with the map {−,?,+}→A⊥\{-,?,+\}\to A_{\bot} which is defined by −↦η(0𝟐)-\mapsto\eta(0_{\mathbf{2}}), ?↦⊥?\mapsto\bot, and +↦η(1𝟐)+\mapsto\eta(1_{\mathbf{2}}). This defines a monotone sequence in 𝟐⊥\mathbf{2}_{\bot}, and we can form ⨆f¯:𝟐⊥\bigsqcup\overline{f}:\mathbf{2}_{\bot} to answer whether ff represents a positive or negative number, or is zero. One can check that equivalent Cauchy sequences get mapped to equal values, hence we get 𝑖𝑠𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑒:ℝq→𝟐⊥\mathit{isPositive}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\to\mathbf{2}_{\bot}.

The strategy outlined above does not quite work for the reals defined as a HIIT [20] because, roughly speaking, in that setting fnf_{n} is a real number and a comparison such as fn⋅n<−2f_{n}\cdot n<-2 is undecidable. Recently Gilbert 2017 has refined our approach and defined a function 𝑖𝑠𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑒\mathit{isPositive} for such reals [13], using the definition of the partiality monad presented in this text (with insignificant differences). Gilbert 2017’s key observation is that comparisons between real numbers and rational numbers are semidecidable, and semidecidability is sufficient to define 𝑖𝑠𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑡𝑖𝑣𝑒\mathit{isPositive}.

6 Discussion and Further Work

We have constructed a partiality monad without using countable choice. This is only a first step in the development of a form of constructive domain theory in type theory. It remains to be seen whether it is possible to, for instance, replicate the work of Benton et al. 2009 [5], who develop domain theory using the delay monad.

Consider the partial function 𝑓𝑖𝑙𝑡𝑒𝑟:ΠA:𝖲𝖾𝗍(A→𝟐)→Aω→Aω\mathit{filter}:\Pi_{A:\mathsf{Set}}\,(A\to\mathbf{2})\to A^{\omega}\to A^{\omega} that filters out elements from a stream. How should partial streams over AA be defined? Defining them as ν​X.(A×X)⊥\nu X.(A\times X)_{\bot} seems inadequate, because the ordering of (−)⊥(-)_{\bot} is flat. One approach would perhaps be to define this type by solving a domain equation. Instead of relying on the type-theoretic mechanism to define recursive types, we can perhaps construct a suitable type of partial streams as the colimit of an ω\omega-cocontinuous functor on the category of ω\omega-cpos. Preliminary investigations indicate that QIITs are useful in the endeavour, for example in the definition of a lifting comonad on ω\omega-cpos (as suggested by Paolo Capriotti).

Going in another direction, it might be worth investigating how much topology can be done using the Sierpinski space, represented as 𝟏⊥\mathbf{1}_{\bot} in our setting. A very similar question was discussed at the Special Year on Univalent Foundations of Mathematics at the IAS in Princeton (2012–2013). Moreover, some observations have been presented by Gilbert 2017 [13], who used our definition of 𝟏⊥\mathbf{1}_{\bot} as presented in this paper (with minor differences).
